\section{The problem}
% =====================================================================

A ride-hailing company runs a fleet of vehicles. Every vehicle sends its position,
speed and status all the time. Once a day, the fuel and garage partners send a file
with what each vehicle cost to run. Dispatch staff want to see the fleet as it is now.
Finance wants to know which vehicles are losing money once those costs are counted.
This project builds one data platform that answers both questions from the same data.
The assignment states the question like this:

\callout{Business question (use case 1)}{What is fleet utilization and earnings by
area and time of day right now, and which vehicles are becoming unprofitable once
yesterday's fuel and maintenance costs are factored in?}

This one sentence holds two questions, and they want opposite things.

\textbf{Question A, ``right now'',} is asked by dispatch, all day, on a wall screen.
The answer must arrive within seconds. A small error does not matter: nobody decides
anything differently because utilization is 71\,\% and not 72\,\%.

\textbf{Question B, ``becoming unprofitable'',} is asked by finance, once a day. The
answer can take hours, and it has to: the partner cost file does not exist until the
day is over. But the answer must be exact, because it is used to settle with drivers.
The word ``becoming'' also makes it a question about a trend over several days, not
about one day.

One question accepts error and needs speed. The other accepts delay and needs
correctness. A single processing path would have to give up one of them. That is the
reason, argued in Section~\ref{sec:arch}, that the system is a Lambda architecture.

\subsection{The two data sources}

Both sources are Python programs that simulate the real thing.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Streaming source: vehicle telemetry.} 150 vehicles move around a grid
  of 12 zones in the city. Each vehicle is \emph{idle}, \emph{on the way} to a pickup,
  or \emph{on a trip}, and demand rises and falls through the day. Every vehicle sends
  one reading every 3 simulated minutes, about \mThroughput{} events per real second.
  \item \textbf{Daily batch source: partner expenses.} One CSV file per simulated day
  with fuel and maintenance cost per vehicle, written straight to object storage and
  never through Kafka. The dropper also produces the awkward cases: a missing file and
  a corrected file for an earlier day.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:d1} shows who sends data into the platform and who uses what comes
out. It names no technology on purpose.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/D1-context.pdf}
\caption{System context. One source is continuous and one is a daily file. Dispatch
gives up some accuracy for speed; finance gives up speed for accuracy.}
\label{fig:d1}
\end{figure}

\section{Lambda or Kappa}
\label{sec:arch}
% =====================================================================

We compared the two architectures on the eight criteria from the module. Six favour
Lambda, including the two we think decide the question. Two favour Kappa, and we
accept both.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|l|X|}
\hline
\rowcolor{tablehead}\hd{Criterion} & \hd{Favours} & \hd{Reason for this use case} \\ \hline
Latency & Lambda & Two latency needs at the same time: seconds for dispatch, a day for finance. The daily delay comes from the partners, not from us. \\ \hline
Data volume & Lambda & 72{,}000 events per simulated day here; a real fleet of 5{,}000 vehicles sends 14.4 million a day, and a trend needs 7 to 30 days of it at rest. \\ \hline
Historical analysis & \textbf{Lambda} & \textbf{Decisive.} ``Becoming unprofitable'' is a trend over many days. Holding weeks of state in a stream job is fragile and is lost on restart. \\ \hline
Reprocessing & \textbf{Lambda} & \textbf{Decisive.} A corrected cost file for one day re-reads one stored day. Under Kappa it means replaying that whole day of telemetry. \\ \hline
Fault tolerance & Lambda & If the stream job fails, only the live screen goes stale. The financial record is rebuilt from stored raw data. \\ \hline
Accuracy & Lambda & The question itself asks for fast and approximate plus slow and exact. \\ \hline
Complexity & Kappa & Accepted. Reduced by one shared transform library and one merge point. \\ \hline
Cost & Kappa & Accepted for the number of containers; not for storage (see below). \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{The eight criteria from the module, applied to this use case.}
\end{table}

\subsection{The two criteria that decide it}

\textbf{Historical analysis.} Finance does not ask which vehicles lost money yesterday.
It asks which vehicles are getting worse. We compute a 7 simulated day rolling net
profit and a least-squares trend line for every vehicle, and label each one
\texttt{HEALTHY}, \texttt{WATCH} or \texttt{UNPROFITABLE}. To do this from a stream, we
would need to keep weeks of state inside the streaming job, or keep weeks of data in
Kafka and replay it. Both bring back, in a worse form, the batch processing Kappa is
meant to remove.

\textbf{Reprocessing.} Partners really do send corrections: a fuel card charged to the
wrong vehicle, a garage bill a day late. Under Lambda, the corrected file lands, the
batch job for that one date runs again, reads one stored day of telemetry, joins it
again and overwrites the rows for that date. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that
running it twice changes nothing. Under Kappa the same correction means replaying every
telemetry event of that day. The cost of a correction should depend on the size of the
correction, not on the size of the biggest dataset.

\subsection{The two criteria we accept}

\textbf{Complexity.} The module names two Lambda drawbacks: two codebases, and keeping
two systems in agreement. There is only one codebase for business logic: every rule
lives in \texttt{src/fleet/transforms/} as plain functions on DataFrames, which the speed
layer calls on each micro-batch and the batch layer on a whole day. They are tested once,
and an architecture test fails if either layer defines its own logic. And the two views
are not hoped to agree: Section~\ref{sec:merge} defines exactly where one stops.

\textbf{Cost.} Lambda runs more containers. But the history is Parquet on object
storage, the cheapest durable storage, while Redis holds only two simulated hours, and
Kafka keeps telemetry for one real hour. A Kappa system that replays 30 days must keep 30
days on the brokers, which costs more per byte. So we accept the cost criterion for
containers, but not for storage.

\subsection{The case for Kappa, and why it lost}

Kappa has a real case. One path, one codebase, and nothing for two views to disagree
about. Fewer containers, no object store, no watermark bookkeeping. The expense file
could even be a stream: publish each row to a compacted topic keyed by vehicle and
date, so that a correction is simply a newer record under the same key, and join it
with telemetry continuously. At our volume, replay would be cheap.

It lost for four reasons. History is the question, not a side feature, and the module
itself says Kappa is weaker at historical analysis. Reprocessing cost points the wrong
way, and corrections happen every week. Profit per vehicle is used to pay drivers, and a
stored file plus the job that produced a figure is a better answer to an auditor than a
replayed log. And a CSV that a garage uploads once a day is not an event stream.

\textbf{What would change our minds.} We would choose Kappa if all three of these
became true: costs arrived continuously (for example from fuel sensors) instead of in
a daily invoice; the trend window became short enough to keep in stream state (hours,
not weeks); and nobody needed to audit the financial figures.

\section{Architecture}
% =====================================================================

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/D2-layered-architecture.pdf}
\caption{The layered architecture, read from top to bottom. Blue is the speed path,
green the batch path and orange the serving layer where the two meet. The daily
expense file goes straight to object storage, not through Kafka.}
\label{fig:d2}
\end{figure}

Four properties of Figure~\ref{fig:d2} carry the design.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One log, several independent readers.} Kafka keeps telemetry as a
  stored log, not a queue. Six streaming queries read the same records, each with its
  own position and checkpoint, so one can fail without stopping the others. A Redis
  outage does not stop the Parquet writes.
  \item \textbf{The raw data is never changed.} Valid telemetry is appended to the lake
  as Parquet, one folder per simulated date. The batch layer only reads from it, so a
  correction is always a recomputation, and any past day can be reproduced.
  \item \textbf{The zone is worked out when reading, not stored.} Telemetry is stored
  with its latitude and longitude, and both layers use the same function to find the
  zone. If the zone grid is redrawn, history can be recomputed with the new grid.
  \item \textbf{The two paths meet in one place only:} the merge in the serving
  layer, described next.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The merge boundary}
\label{sec:merge}

The batch layer keeps a one-row table, \texttt{batch\_high\_water\_mark}, with the last
simulated date it has fully processed. The serving layer reads it for every request
and applies one rule:

\callout{The merge rule}{The batch view answers every date up to and
\textbf{including} the high-water mark. The speed view answers every date
\textbf{after} it. So each date belongs to exactly one side: no date is counted twice
and none is lost. Every row says which side produced it (\texttt{source}) and whether
it is exact (\texttt{exact}).}

The high-water mark moves inside the same database transaction as the facts it
describes, so it can never point at a day whose data has not been saved.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{figures/D4-merge-boundary.pdf}
\caption{The merge rule. (a) Normal case: batch up to yesterday, speed for today.
(b) Batch three days behind: the days in between are listed as uncovered, not
silently dropped.}
\label{fig:d4}
\end{figure}

Two behaviours were added after testing. First, Redis only holds the current window,
not a history per date. If the batch layer falls more than one day behind, some dates
are answered by neither side. Returning fewer rows without a word would make a stalled
pipeline look like a quiet week, so the response lists those dates in
\texttt{uncovered\_dates} and says why (Figure~\ref{fig:d4}b). Second, a date in the
future is reported as ``not simulated yet'', not as ``batch is behind'', so nobody goes
looking for a fault that does not exist.

\section{Technology choices}
% =====================================================================

Each choice is justified for this use case, together with what we gave up for it.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\scriptsize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|l|X|X|}
\hline
\rowcolor{tablehead}\hd{Layer} & \hd{Chosen} & \hd{Why, for this use case} & \hd{What we gave up} \\ \hline
Ingestion & Kafka (KRaft) & Order is kept per vehicle by keying on \texttt{vehicle\_id}; one log serves many readers & One broker, so no fault tolerance \\ \hline
Serialization & Avro and Schema Registry & The schema will change; much smaller than JSON & Harder to read by eye; one more container \\ \hline
Speed processing & Spark Structured Streaming & Same engine as the batch layer, so one shared transform library & Micro-batch delay \\ \hline
Speed store & Redis & Key lookups only; built-in expiry keeps the view small & Not durable, on purpose \\ \hline
Raw data store & MinIO and Parquet & Files are never changed, so a recompute gives the same result; column pruning & No transactions on the lake \\ \hline
Batch processing & Spark (batch) & Shares code with the speed layer; handles the wide join & JVM start-up for each run \\ \hline
Batch store & PostgreSQL star schema & Transactions for money; window functions for the rolling trend & Holds only aggregates \\ \hline
Orchestration & Airflow & File checks, retries, branching, and a UI that shows health & The heaviest component \\ \hline
Serving & FastAPI & A place for the merge rule to live and be tested & Our own code to maintain \\ \hline
Dashboards & Grafana & Set up from files, so a fresh clone gets the same dashboards & Less custom than a hand-made UI \\ \hline
Metrics and alerts & Prometheus, Pushgateway, Alertmanager & The required alert rules are one line of PromQL each; real routing and inhibition & Pushgateway keeps old values (Section~\ref{sec:obs}) \\ \hline
Logging & \texttt{structlog} to JSON & Every log line has the same fields & \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Technology stack.}
\end{table}

\textbf{Spark} was chosen over Storm, Flink or Kafka Streams not for speed (any of them
handles 240 events per second) but because it runs the \emph{same code} in streaming and
batch mode, which is how we keep one shared transform library. \textbf{Redis} was chosen
partly because it is not durable: the live view must be disposable, and expiry is a
property of the store. \textbf{Parquet on object storage} holds the raw data because it
must never change; append-only files make ``recompute that day'' the only way to
correct anything.

MinIO, Parquet, Prometheus, Grafana and Alertmanager were not covered in the module.
We added them on purpose and say so here.

\section{Implementation}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Ingestion}

\textbf{The partition key is the most important choice in this layer.} The idle
detector keeps state per vehicle and must see \emph{on trip}, then \emph{idle}, then
\emph{idle} in that order. Kafka only keeps order inside one partition, so the key is
\texttt{vehicle\_id}: all readings of one vehicle go to one partition and arrive in
order. Any other key would break the feature.

Messages are Avro, registered in the Schema Registry, in the Confluent wire format (a
magic byte and a four-byte schema id in front of each message), so producers and
consumers can change separately. A decoded message is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:kafka-telemetry-messages} in the appendix.

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{|l|l|X|}
\hline
\rowcolor{tablehead}\hd{Topic} & \hd{Partitions and policy} & \hd{Purpose} \\ \hline
\texttt{fleet.telemetry.v1} & 6, delete after 1 real hour & Vehicle readings, keyed by vehicle \\ \hline
\texttt{fleet.telemetry.dlq} & 1, delete after 14 days & Rejected readings, original fields and the reason \\ \hline
\texttt{fleet.alerts.v1} & 3, delete after 7 days & Idle-vehicle alerts from the speed layer \\ \hline
\texttt{fleet.vehicle.registry} & 3, compacted & One record per vehicle, newest kept \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Kafka topics.}
\end{table}

About 1\,\% of readings are broken on purpose: negative fares, missing coordinates,
impossible speeds, positions outside the city and timestamps in the future. They are
not decoration. They are the known input that lets us check the dead-letter path
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\shot{kafka-dlq-messages}{0.72}{Kafka UI: the dead-letter topic. Each record keeps the
original values and the reason it was rejected.}

\subsection{Speed layer}

One Spark Structured Streaming job runs six queries on the telemetry topic, each with
its own checkpoint, every 10 real seconds: the raw data writer, the dead-letter
writer, zone activity, zone earnings, the idle detector, and live state per vehicle.

Two details matter. \emph{Activity and earnings are separate queries}, because a
streaming query allows only one aggregation. And \emph{earnings must be computed from
one row per trip}: the fare is repeated on every reading of a trip, so summing it
directly multiplies revenue by the number of readings. This was found only by comparing
total revenue with the number of distinct trips; nothing raised an error. The running
queries and one zone of the live view in Redis are in Figures~\ref{fig:spark-streaming}
and~\ref{fig:terminal-redis-speed-view} in the appendix.

\subsection{Storage}

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/D5-storage.pdf}
\caption{The three stores: Redis keys for the live view, the Parquet lake split by
simulated date, and the PostgreSQL star schema for the batch view.}
\label{fig:d5}
\end{figure}

The lake keeps only valid readings, one folder per simulated date, so the batch job for
one day reads exactly one folder. PostgreSQL holds the exact results in a star schema:
a vehicle dimension, a daily P\&L fact table keyed by vehicle and date, an hourly zone
fact table, and the one-row high-water mark. Figure~\ref{fig:minio-lake} in the appendix
shows the date folders in the lake.

\subsection{Batch layer and orchestration}

Two Spark jobs run for each simulated day. The zone job computes exact hourly figures
per zone with an exact distinct count, where the speed layer uses an approximate one;
both sit in the same source file, so the trade-off is easy to see. The profitability
job rebuilds trips, measures distance from our own GPS trace (haversine between
consecutive positions), joins the expense file, and computes the 7-day rolling average
and trend. The join keeps every case: vehicles with costs, vehicles with no cost row,
and cost rows with no telemetry.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/D6-daily-batch.pdf}
\caption{The daily DAG. A corrupt expense file is moved to quarantine; a missing one
is recorded, and the P\&L still runs with those vehicles marked as missing costs.}
\label{fig:d6}
\end{figure}

One Airflow DAG, \texttt{fleet\_daily\_reconciliation}, checks every real minute and
batches each simulated day once, about a minute after the day ends; the other runs stop
at the first task because the day is already done. It takes the date from the simulated
clock, not from Airflow's own date (the two are unrelated, and using Airflow's would
process the wrong folder on every run). It saves a snapshot of the speed view, checks the expense file,
branches on the result, runs both Spark jobs, checks that the high-water mark moved,
and records the difference between the speed and batch views. The DAG file contains
no business logic; a test that parses it fails if any appears. The runs are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:airflow-grid} and the task graph in Figure~\ref{fig:airflow-graph}.

\subsection{Serving layer}

A FastAPI service is the only way to read the data. It reads Redis and PostgreSQL
through connection pools, applies the merge rule, and says in every answer which rows
are exact and which are approximate. Grafana reads through the same API, not from the
stores directly, so a broken panel is a true report about the serving layer.

\shot{terminal-api-merged}{0.7}{The merged answer for a range of dates. Dates up to
the high-water mark come from the batch view, later dates from the speed view.}

\subsection{Consumer lag does not work for Structured Streaming}

The usual way to watch a Kafka consumer is consumer-group lag. \textbf{It does not work
here.} Structured Streaming keeps its offsets in its own checkpoint and never commits
them to a consumer group, so the lag reads zero whether a query is keeping up or has
been dead for hours. On the running system, listing consumer groups returns none at
all. An earlier attempt to force it with \texttt{kafka.group.id} stopped the
dead-letter query after about 4{,}500 micro-batches.

What works is the \textbf{age of the last progress report} pushed by a Spark listener.
A query that processed zero rows may be healthy and idle; a query that has stopped
reports nothing, and only the age of the last report tells the two apart. Pushgateway
keeps the last value after the job dies, so we alert on the age of the timestamp, not
on its value.

\subsection{An alert that could not fire}

The brief asks for an alert when no data has arrived for some minutes. Our first
version was:

\begin{center}\ttfamily\small
sum(rate(events\_produced\_total[2m])) == 0
\end{center}

It loaded without error. We then stopped the producer, and the alert stayed inactive
for six and a half minutes. The reason: when the producer dies, its metrics disappear,
and a sum over nothing is not zero but an empty result, which never equals zero. The
alert for ``no data'' was defeated by having no data. The fix adds \texttt{or
vector(0)}, plus a second rule on \texttt{up == 0}, a series Prometheus always keeps
for every target. The measured times after the fix are in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Inhibition}

Stopping the producer makes several rules true at once: the producer target is down,
no telemetry arrives, and the streaming queries stall. Only the first is the cause.
Alertmanager inhibition hides the consequences so the person on call sees the cause.
Our first inhibition rule hid nothing, because it matched \texttt{severity="warning"}
while the alerts it should hide are \texttt{critical}. It now follows the chain from
cause to effect. Every scrape target is shown up in Figure~\ref{fig:prometheus-targets}.

\section{Limitations and lessons}
\label{sec:limits}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Every bug we found was silent}

\textbf{Not one bug in this project announced itself.} None raised an exception, none
appeared in a log, and each one would have survived a demonstration that only asked
``is it running?''. Earlier examples: revenue was 8.5 times too high because the fare repeats on every
reading of a trip; the speed layer started only two of its six queries, so Redis was
simply empty; an Airflow branch skipped the whole batch path and the DAG went amber, not
red; and the Airflow web UI was down for three hours while the scheduler kept running.

The final clean runs for this report found eight more, most of them by comparing an
output with a number we already knew:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Vehicles drove out of the city.} More ``outside the city'' readings were
  dead-lettered than injected: pickup points and position noise were not kept inside the
  area the validator accepts. They now are, with a test over a whole simulated day.
  \item \textbf{The future-timestamp check moved with the load.} It used the micro-batch
  clock, which is early or late by however far processing is behind: 98 honest readings
  were rejected in one run, and 15 of 480 injected faults passed in another. Readings are
  now compared with the time they were sent; honest ones are within one simulated
  minute of it, the injected fault two hours ahead.
  \item \textbf{A skipped snapshot skipped the whole day.} With no zones in the speed view
  yet, the snapshot step skipped, and the default trigger rule passed the skip down the
  batch path. The branch now runs unless something actually failed.
  \item \textbf{Yesterday was answered by neither view.} On a fixed 5 minute timer, every
  DAG run started just before the simulated midnight, so the high-water mark stayed two
  days behind for all but about 15 seconds of each day. The DAG now checks every minute;
  over 20 minutes the mark was at yesterday in \mHwmHealthy{} of samples.
  \item \textbf{The trip de-duplication never forgot a trip.} Spark only clears that state
  when the time column is in the key. It now uses \texttt{dropDuplicatesWithinWatermark},
  with a streaming test that old trips are cleared.
  \item \textbf{A Redis outage stopped the whole speed layer.} A Redis error inside a
  writer ended its query, the job stopped, and the container restarted in a loop, which
  also paused the lake writes. The writers now skip the batch and count the error.
  \item \textbf{A false alarm after every outage.} Spark's idle message has the query's
  id but not its name, so heartbeats were sent as ``unnamed'' and raised a stall alert
  once data resumed. The listener now keeps each query's name.
  \item \textbf{Two dashboards showed ``No data''.} An unpinned Grafana plugin updated
  to a version our Grafana cannot load, and the screenshot script still reported success.
  The plugin is pinned and the script now rejects ``No data''.
\end{itemize}

After these fixes, the dead-lettered counts in Table~\ref{tab:dlq} match the injected
ones, apart from faults sent in the second before the producer stopped.
The lesson we take from this project: \textbf{a pipeline that merely runs tells you
nothing; a pipeline whose output can be checked against a number you already know
tells you a lot.} The same applies to the checks themselves. Our screenshot script
once reported success for a login page, the alert rule above loaded and could never
fire, and a DAG test passed against zero DAGs. Each was fixed by making the check test
something it could get wrong.

\subsection{Simplifications}

\textbf{Scale.} One Kafka broker with replication factor 1; telemetry kept in Kafka for
one real hour; Spark in local mode with four cores (windows, watermarks, checkpoints and
state behave as on a cluster, but work is not spread over machines); 150 vehicles; no
authentication or TLS, and default passwords. At 240 events per second this is not a
test of scale; it shows that the architecture is correct and how the pipeline behaves.

\textbf{Correctness.} The system is at-least-once, so every writer overwrites instead of
adding. Speed-layer distinct counts are approximate (HyperLogLog, about 2\,\% error).
Live earnings lag by up to one trip, because a fare counts when the trip ends. Readings
later than the 30 simulated minute watermark are left out of the live windows but still
reach the lake. The lake has no transactions, so a crashed writer can leave a partial
file.

\subsection{The speed and batch views differ more than we predicted}

Every day the DAG saves a snapshot of the speed view and later compares it with the
batch figures for the same hour. We predicted a difference of 1 to 3\,\% on counts and
up to 8\,\% on earnings. Over \mReconDays{} days, the idle ratio agreed within
\mReconIdle{}, but trips per minute read \mReconTrips{} low and earnings per minute
\mReconEarn{} low.

Two real bugs were fixed on the way to these numbers. The first comparison put a 15
minute sliding window next to a whole batch day. The second matched rows by when
Airflow ran, not by the time window the data described. What remains is not a fault
in either layer: the snapshot catches a sliding window that is still filling, so counts
and earnings read low, while a ratio such as the idle ratio does not depend on how full
the window is. That is why the idle ratio agrees and the counts do not. The real check
that the two layers compute the same thing is the revenue comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Making counts comparable would need the live view to keep a
full history per hour, which we leave as future work.

\subsection{What is not implemented}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Distributed tracing.} The design planned OpenTelemetry and Jaeger; it
  was cut for time. Observability rests on logs, metrics and alerts.
  \item \textbf{A PDF daily report.} The brief accepts ``a consolidated report or
  dashboard''; the Grafana dashboards meet it.
  \item \textbf{Automatic corrections.} The expense dropper writes a marker when it
  sends a corrected file, but nothing reads it. A correction is applied by running
  \texttt{make dag-trigger DATE=...} for that date.
  \item \textbf{Catching up missed days.} The DAG does not run days missed during an
  outage (\texttt{catchup} is off); \texttt{make backfill} does that instead.
\end{itemize}

The most important simplification: the expense file is generated from \emph{our own}
simulated distance, so the two sources are not truly independent. A real cost source
could disagree in ways this simulation cannot produce, and the reconciliation results
should be read with that in mind.

\subsection{At production scale}

Kafka with three replicas, TLS, authentication and schema checks in CI; Spark on
Kubernetes with a RocksDB state store and \emph{separate clusters for speed and batch},
so a batch job cannot slow the live view; Delta Lake or Iceberg on the lake, so a
correction becomes a \texttt{MERGE} with full history; data quality tests with
quarantine; and access control, lineage and GDPR handling for driver data, which a
system that computes profit per driver clearly needs.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

The system takes in two different sources, processes them on a speed path and a batch
path that share one tested transform library, joins the two at one clear boundary,
and shows its own health. Both halves of the business question are answered from live
data: utilization and earnings by area within seconds, and a ranked list of vehicles
becoming unprofitable once partner costs are counted.

We chose Lambda because the question has two halves with opposite needs, and because a
corrected invoice should cost work in proportion to the correction, not to a whole day
of telemetry. Six of the eight criteria support that choice; the two that do not are
accepted and reduced in the code.

The main lesson: every bug we found was silent, and the one method that found them
was checking an output against a number we already knew. Two independent calculations
of the same revenue agreeing to the cent say more about whether this pipeline is
correct than any number of green dashboard panels.
